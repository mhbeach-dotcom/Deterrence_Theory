Block I: Foundations of Coercion, Security Dilemma, & Perception

WEEK 01 Coercion and Deterrence Monday, 24 August 2026 · Seminar 106 Pages 2 Readings + 1 Video

“The power to hurt is bargaining power. To exploit it is diplomacy—vicious diplomacy, but diplomacy.”

— Thomas C. Schelling, Arms and Influence

Focus & Overview

Introduces foundational concepts in coercion, deterrence, and compellence. Sets the theoretical baseline for how military power is used as a instrument of diplomatic pressure and strategic restraint.

Learning Objectives

  • Understand key terms, foundational concepts, and the underlying logic of deterrence and coercion in international politics.
  • Distinguish between deterrence (preventing action) and compellence (forcing action) as core components of coercion theory.
  • Analyze how the threat and controlled use of force function as instruments of bargaining.

Discussion Questions

  • What is the fundamental difference between coercion by deterrence and coercion by compellence, according to Schelling and Biddle?
  • How does the "power to hurt" translate into bargaining power in crisis diplomacy?
  • Why is compellence generally considered more difficult to achieve than deterrence?

Required Reading & Media

  • Schelling, Thomas C. Arms and Influence. New Haven: Yale University Press, 1966, pp. 1–91. (91 pages)
  • Biddle, Tami Davis. "Coercion Theory: A Basic Introduction for Practitioners." Texas National Security Review 3, no. 2 (Spring 2020): 94–109. (15 pages)
  • Video: "Coercion & Deterrence: A Primer." YouTube, 25:42 mins.
WEEK 02 Security Dilemma, Perception, and Misperception Monday, 31 August 2026 · Seminar (PDF syllabus listed Wed 2 Sept — Blackboard now shows Mon 31 Aug) 84 Pages 4 Readings

“Actors often fail to realize that actions taken to increase their own security may threaten others, initiating a security dilemma.”

— Robert Jervis

Focus & Overview

Examines psychological and cognitive dimensions of decision-making, threat perception, emotion, and how defensive measures can provoke spiraling insecurity. (Note: Blackboard now schedules this session for Monday, 31 August 2026, rather than the Wednesday, 2 September date shown in the original PDF syllabus — confirm in Blackboard.)

Learning Objectives

  • Examine how cognition, psychological biases, and emotion shape threat perception and decision-making.
  • Analyze the dynamics of the security dilemma and evaluate methods to distinguish defensive preparations from offensive intent.
  • Assess real-world cases (e.g., Saddam Hussein's Iraq) where misperception led to deterrence breakdown or unintended conflict.

Discussion Questions

  • How do psychological biases and emotional factors distort state leaders' perception of foreign intentions?
  • In what ways can a state's attempt to enhance its own deterrence accidentally trigger a security dilemma?
  • What insights does Saddam Hussein's internal decision-making reveal regarding how authoritarian leaders process deterrence signals?

Required Reading

  • Stein, Janice Gross. "Perceiving Threat: Cognition, Emotion, and Judgment." In The Oxford Handbook of Political Psychology, 3rd ed., edited by Leonie Huddy et al., 392–415. Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2023. (23 pages)
  • Jervis, Robert. "Deterrence and Perception." In How Statesmen Think: The Psychology of International Politics, Ch. 9. Princeton: Princeton University Press, 2017. (25 pages)
  • Glaser, Charles. "Fear Factor: How to Know When You're In a Security Dilemma." Foreign Affairs (July/August 2024): 122–128. (7 pages)
  • Woods, Kevin, James Lacey, and Williamson Murray. "Saddam's Delusions: The View From the Inside." Foreign Affairs (May/June 2006): 26–49. (29 pages)

Block II: Bargaining, Costly Signaling, & Conventional Deterrence

WEEK 03 Signaling, Escalation, and Instruments of National Power Wednesday, 9 September 2026 · Seminar (shifted from Mon 7 Sept — Labor Day) 51 Pages 4 Readings Paper Proposal Due 9 Sept

“To convey resolve, threats must be costly: states can either tie their hands or sink costs.”

— James D. Fearon

Focus & Overview

Analyzes how states communicate resolve, manage escalation, and utilize bargaining models of war to establish credible deterrence. Session meets Wednesday, 9 September 2026 (shifted from the usual Monday because 7 Sept is Labor Day). 2-page paper proposal due Wed, 9 September 2026, 11:59 PM EDT (the PDF syllabus listed 7 Sept; Blackboard's Assignment entry confirms 9 Sept).

Learning Objectives

  • Understand the rationalist bargaining framework of war and how incomplete information creates incentives to misrepresent.
  • Compare mechanisms of signaling resolve: "tying hands" (audience costs) versus "sinking costs."
  • Evaluate the strategic reputational consequences and audience costs associated with empty threats.

Discussion Questions

  • How do states overcome the problem of cheap talk when signaling intentions during a crisis?
  • What is the difference between "tying hands" and "sinking costs" in crisis bargaining?
  • Are audience costs real in practice, or is the cost of empty threats overblown in international relations theory?

Required Reading

  • Reiter, Dan. "Exploring the Bargaining Model of War." Perspectives on Politics 1, no. 1 (2003): 27–43. (16 pages)
  • Fearon, James. "Signaling Foreign Policy Interests: Tying Hands Versus Sinking Costs." Journal of Conflict Resolution 41, no. 1 (1997): 68–72, 82–87 (skim 72–82). (11 pages)
  • Snyder, Jack, and Erica D. Borghard. "The Cost of Empty Threats: A Penny, Not a Pound." American Political Science Review 105, no. 3 (2011): 437–455. (19 pages)
  • Casler, Don, and Keren Yarhi-Milo. "Signaling, Resolve, and Reputation in International Politics." In The Oxford Handbook of Political Psychology, 3rd ed., 426–440. (15 pages)
WEEK 04 Conventional Deterrence Monday, 14 September 2026 · Seminar 60 Pages 4 Readings

“Conventional deterrence depends on persuading an adversary that offensive military operations will be denied or made prohibitively costly.”

— John J. Mearsheimer

Focus & Overview

Explores deterrence by denial versus punishment using non-nuclear military forces, precision munitions, and conventional retaliation strategies.

Learning Objectives

  • Distinguish between deterrence by denial and deterrence by punishment in conventional warfare settings.
  • Analyze the operational impact of technology, such as precision-guided munitions (PGMs), on conventional deterrence stability.
  • Evaluate historical and contemporary conventional deterrence challenges in regional theaters (e.g., Europe, Western Pacific).

Discussion Questions

  • Why is conventional deterrence inherently less stable or predictable than nuclear deterrence?
  • How do precision-guided munitions alter an aggressor's calculation of military success?
  • Is conventional retaliation a credible alternative to nuclear response options during high-intensity conflict?

Required Reading

  • Mazarr, Michael J. "Understanding Deterrence." RAND Perspectives (2018): 1–14. (14 pages)
  • Mearsheimer, John J. "Precision-Guided Munitions and Conventional Deterrence." Survival 21, no. 2 (1979): 68–76. (8 pages)
  • Huntington, Samuel P. "Conventional Deterrence and Conventional Retaliation in Europe." International Security 8, no. 3 (Winter 1983/84): 32–56. (24 pages)
  • Mueller, Karl. "The Continuing Relevance of Conventional Deterrence." In NL ARMS Netherlands Annual Review of Military Studies 2020, edited by F. Osinga and T. Sweijs, 47–61. Asser Press, 2021. (14 pages)

Block III: Nuclear Strategy, Proliferation, & Regional Dynamics

WEEK 05 The Nuclear Weapons Debate (Part I) Monday, 21 September 2026 · Seminar 106 Pages 5 Readings

“When both sides have a counterforce capability, nuclear superiority does not yield diplomatic leverage.”

— Robert Jervis

Focus & Overview

Debates the role of nuclear force posture, parity vs. superiority, brinkmanship, and the effectiveness of nuclear coercive diplomacy.

Learning Objectives

  • Contrast theoretical perspectives on nuclear superiority versus existential deterrence (Jervis vs. Kroenig).
  • Evaluate empirical evidence regarding whether nuclear weapons enable coercive diplomacy or primary deterrence.
  • Examine how modern counterforce technology affects nuclear force posture and crisis stability.

Discussion Questions

  • Does nuclear superiority provide meaningful political or military leverage in international crises?
  • Why do Sechser and Fuhrmann express skepticism regarding the utility of nuclear coercion?
  • How does modern counterforce technology challenge traditional concepts of Mutually Assured Destruction (MAD)?

Required Reading

  • Fuhrmann, Matthew. "Nuclear Weapons." In What Do We Know About War?, edited by Sara McLaughlin Mitchell and John A. Vasquez, 3rd ed., 103–119. Lanham, MD: Rowman & Littlefield, 2024. (17 pages)
  • Jervis, Robert. "Why Nuclear Superiority Doesn't Matter." Political Science Quarterly 94, no. 4 (Winter 1979–80): 617–633. (16 pages)
  • Sechser, Todd, and Matthew Fuhrmann. "Nuclear Coercion and Nuclear Skepticism." In Nuclear Weapons and Coercive Diplomacy, Ch. 2. Cambridge University Press, 2017. (39 pages)
  • Kroenig, Matthew. "Toward a New Theory of Nuclear Deterrence: The Superiority-Brinkmanship Synthesis Theory." In The Logic of American Nuclear Security, Ch. 1. Columbia University Press, 2018. (22 pages)
  • Lieber, Keir A., and Daryl G. Press. "The New Era of Nuclear Weapons, Deterrence, and Conflict." Strategic Studies Quarterly 10, no. 5 (2016): 31–42. (12 pages)
WEEK 06 Special Topics in Nuclear Weapons Monday, 28 September 2026 · Seminar 89 Pages 4 Readings

“Organizational biases within military bureaucracies can destabilize nuclear deterrence and increase proliferation risks.”

— Scott D. Sagan

Focus & Overview

Addresses nonproliferation organizational theory, extended nuclear umbrellas, alliance dynamics, and psychological drivers like revenge.

Learning Objectives

  • Compare organizational theory perspectives against deterrence theory on nuclear proliferation risks.
  • Analyze the mechanics and credibility challenges of extended nuclear deterrence and alliance commitments.
  • Explore psychological dynamics, such as the psychology of revenge, in strategic signaling and deterrence.

Discussion Questions

  • Why might professional military organizations handle nuclear weapons less rationally than theoretical models assume?
  • How can a patron state make its extended nuclear deterrence guarantees credible to allies without triggering abandonment or entrapment?
  • What role does the human desire for revenge play in restoring deterrence after a surprise attack?

Required Reading

  • Sagan, Scott D. "The Perils of Proliferation: Organizational Theory, Deterrence Theory, and the Spread of Nuclear Weapons." International Security 18, no. 4 (1994): 66–107. (41 pages)
  • McDermott, Rose, Anthony C. Lopez, and Peter K. Hatemi. "'Blunt Not the Heart, Enrage It': The Psychology of Revenge and Deterrence." Texas National Security Review 1, no. 1 (2017): 68–88. (20 pages)
  • Beckley, Michael. "The Myth of Entangling Alliances." War on the Rocks (June 9, 2015). (5 pages)
  • Fuhrmann, Matthew. "On Extended Nuclear Deterrence." Diplomacy & Statecraft 29, no. 1 (2018): 51–73. (23 pages)
WEEK 07 Nuclear Weapons and China Monday, 5 October 2026 · Seminar 110 Pages 3 Readings + 1 Handout

“Conventional escalation in the Western Pacific risks unintentionally crossing Chinese nuclear thresholds.”

— Caitlin Talmadge

Focus & Overview

Focuses on Chinese strategic stability, nuclear modernization, escalation dynamics in a Western Pacific scenario, and includes "The Brinkmanship Game" practical exercise.

Learning Objectives

  • Understand Chinese doctrines on nuclear posture, strategic stability, and escalation management.
  • Identify potential pathways for conventional-to-nuclear escalation in a major U.S.-China conflict over Taiwan.
  • Apply brinkmanship theory to competitive crisis interactions using "The Brinkmanship Game" exercise.

Discussion Questions

  • How do Chinese views of strategic stability differ from traditional Western cold war concepts?
  • What conventional U.S. military operations could Beijing interpret as a threat to its strategic nuclear deterrent?
  • How does China's nuclear modernization affect its willingness to take risks in regional conventional disputes?

Required Reading & Handout

  • Cunningham, Fiona S., and M. Taylor Fravel. "Dangerous Confidence? Chinese Views on Nuclear Escalation." International Security 44, no. 2 (2019): 61–109. (49 pages)

Block IV: Coercive Air Power, Cyber, & Cross-Domain Deterrence

WEEK 08 Air Power (Vietnam, Kosovo) Wednesday, 14 October 2026 · Seminar (shifted from Mon 12 Oct — Columbus Day/Indigenous Peoples’ Day) 106 Pages 3 Readings + 2 Videos

“Coercion seeks to affect the enemy's will by manipulating the cost-benefit calculus of resistance.”

— Robert A. Pape, Bombing to Win

Focus & Overview

Examines coercive air campaigns, denial versus punishment strategies in Vietnam and Kosovo, and the effectiveness of air power in achieving political concessions. Session meets Wednesday, 14 October 2026 (shifted from the usual Monday because 12 Oct is Columbus Day/Indigenous Peoples’ Day).

Learning Objectives

  • Compare Pape's strategies of coercion: punishment, risk, denial, and decapitation.
  • Analyze historical air campaigns (Rolling Thunder, Linebacker I/II, Operation Allied Force) to evaluate coercive efficacy.
  • Examine how adversary military strategy (e.g., conventional vs. guerrilla) affects air power success.

Discussion Questions

  • Why does Pape argue that denial strategies are far more effective than punishment strategies in coercive air power?
  • How did Linebacker II differ from Rolling Thunder in its coercive logic and military execution?
  • What lessons from NATO's air campaign in Kosovo apply to modern multi-domain coercion?

Required Reading & Media

  • Pape, Robert A. Bombing to Win: Air Power and Coercion in War. Ithaca, NY: Cornell University Press, 1996, pp. 1–11, 55–86, 174–195. (63 pages)
  • Haun, Phil, and Colin Jackson. "Breakers of Armies: Air Power in the Easter Offensive and the Myth of Linebacker I and II in the Vietnam War." International Security 40, no. 3 (Winter 2015/16): 139–150, 160–178. (29 pages)
  • Stigler, Andrew. "Hoping for Victory: Coercive Air Power and NATO's Strategy in Kosovo." In Air Power in the Age of Primacy, edited by Phil Haun, Colin Jackson, and Tim Schultz, 64–78. Cambridge University Press, 2021. (14 pages)

WEEK 09 Emerging Technology, Simultaneity, & Modern Deterrence Challenges Monday, 19 October 2026 · Seminar 102 Pages 4 Readings Final Paper Due 23 Oct

“Cross-domain deterrence links military, cyber, and economic actions across multiple domains to shape adversary behavior.”

— Jon R. Lindsay & Erik Gartzke

Focus & Overview

Addresses cross-domain deterrence, cyber warfare, deterrence by denial in cyberspace, multi-theater deterrence, and emerging technologies. Session meets Monday, 19 October 2026. 2,500-word (minimum) final research paper due Friday, 23 October 2026, 11:59 PM EDT — papers may be submitted early if they conflict with core course assignments.

Learning Objectives

  • Comprehend concepts and trade-offs of cross-domain deterrence across space, cyber, and conventional domains.
  • Evaluate feasibility and mechanics of deterrence by denial in cyberspace.
  • Analyze challenges of multi-theater deterrence and simultaneity in U.S. global defense strategy.

Discussion Questions

  • How does cross-domain deterrence complicate attribution and escalation management?
  • Can cyber capabilities effectively deter physical military aggression, or vice versa?
  • How can the United States break the "double bind" of managing simultaneous multi-theater crises with bounded resources?
